Purpose

The group's research question: "Which systemic barriers hold back the adaptation to increasing salinisation and freshwater scarcity of arable farming in Schouwen-Duiveland towards 2050, and which levers could the actors in this system use to overcome these barriers?" The CLD is the system-wide map of its first part, and with it of the second sub-question: which feedback loops, trade-offs and socio-economic conditions drive or hold back the island's farmers in adopting adaptive practices as soil and water get saltier and fresh water scarcer, and why farms facing similar salt and water conditions respond differently from one another. The responses in the model are: carrying on with the current crops and drainage, storing fresh water on the farm, growing salt-tolerant crops, bringing in fresh water from outside, and land leaving arable farming. Which feedback loops make one response more likely than another, and for whom? Storing fresh water and growing salt-tolerant crops are the adaptive practices in the model because each works on a different part of the chain (the water supply, the crop's sensitivity) and each is held back by something in the system; a practice that farmers raise in the group model building session and that fits neither is a candidate to add.

The CLD is the first step of the study. In the group model building session on the island, farmers and regional stakeholders build their own map of the subsystems from scratch; comparing it with this one shows what we missed or misjudged, and the two are combined. The combined CLD then goes into the semi-quantitative analysis (D2D), which compares single policy levers and combinations of them by their effect on an outcome and traces their unintended effects, and into the multi-criteria analysis of the policy options. It is not built around one outcome or one set of levers: the group picks those when that step starts.

Spatial scale

The island of Schouwen-Duiveland, the island of Zeeland the research zooms in on to keep it feasible: its farmland polders, the rainwater lenses under them, the fresh groundwater under its creek ridges and dunes, the soils, the farms and the land market, and the decisions of the bodies that act there (the water board's water levels, levy and rules on pumping, the province's supply route, the municipality's land-use plans, the banks' lending, the buyers and processors of its crops). The island gets no fresh water from elsewhere, so all its fresh water is winter rain stored in the lenses. What is decided at national and EU level (LVVN, IenW and Rijkswaterstaat, the Deltaprogramma Zoetwater, EU banking rules) comes from outside the boundary: it enters as a Constant or a policy input, and the CLD shows how it reaches the island.

Time horizon

2050, the year the KNMI'23 climate scenarios and the project look ahead to. The slow parts of the system are judged against that date: the freshwater lenses, which a year's recharge barely moves, and land subsidence, which lowers the polders by millimetres a year. The time unit is a year, for every part of the CLD. Each link's description says how long its effect takes to show, from a source, in bands: within the year, years, a decade or more, or beyond 2050; a link slower than a year carries a delay. The seasons sit inside the year, named in the variables they shape (hotter, drier summers; wetter winters; winter rain drained away). A variable that something outside the island sets, or that moves too little by 2050 to matter, is a Constant.

System boundary

The test, from the Purpose (the group's): inside if the farms' responses move it and it moves them back before 2050; outside if it moves the responses and the island cannot move it; left out if it does not move the responses by 2050. Inside the model (endogenous, explained by its loops): the water and salt system (recharge, the rainwater lenses of the low polders and the lens under the creek ridges and dunes, seepage, upconing, salt in the root zone, soil structure); crop yield and farm finances (revenue, land value, debt, credit, room to invest); the farmers' decisions (the drainage they ask for, pumping, storing water, new crops and tolerant varieties, spending on keeping the current crops); and the decisions of the other actors inside the scale: the water board's ditch levels, levy and rules on pumping, the banks' lending, the processing capacity for new crops, the province's supply route and the municipality's land-use plans, with the objections and the trust around plans for the island's water and land. Outside the model (exogenous: they drive it, the model does not explain them): the climate (hotter, drier summers, wetter winters, sea-level rise), EU banking rules (Basel IV), market demand for alternative crops, world prices of conventional crops (what carrying on is weighed against), the growing and processing of new crops elsewhere, the salt-tolerant varieties on offer, national water policy and LVVN's levers. The arable land in the polders is in the model as a stock, which falls when land changes hands to a holder who does not farm it as arable land, because the land market and succession drive it and water management follows it; what the land becomes afterwards is not. Left out: nature and energy, and other land uses (livestock, vertical farming), which replace arable farming rather than adapt it; wisselpolders, because the island's waters carry too little silt to raise its polders (see Polder surface elevation); and salt intrusion into estuaries and irrigation with brackish water, because no water reaches the island's ditches from outside and no source read shows its farmers irrigating with brackish water. We assume the adaptive practices work rather than model whether they do: the CLD shows what stops farmers adopting them, not whether each one works on the island.

Assumptions

  • Each variable is a total for the whole island, and the island's arable farmers are one actor; a group of farms gets its own variables only where a link works differently for it and a source shows that on the island.
  • Each variable measures one quantity, named in its description, so the sign of every link into it can be read.
  • Each actor inside the spatial scale enters the CLD through the variables it decides, with the drivers it responds to; the actors outside it act on the island from outside.
  • A link that claims why an actor decides as it does, and has no source yet, is a hypothesis for the group model building session on the island: its panel says so, and the drawing does not mark it. Every other link is to rest on a source.
  • A rate measured elsewhere in the delta holds for the island only where its place shares the mechanism's driver: the island borders the Oosterschelde and the Grevelingen, whose tide and silt differ from the Westerschelde's.
  • The adaptive practices are assumed to work: the CLD shows what stops farmers adopting them, not whether each one works on the island.
  • The labels and descriptions describe what happens in words each actor in it would accept, and no variable is marked as anyone's lever: which outcome and which levers the analysis looks at is the group's choice for that step.

Simplifications

The CLD leaves detail out on purpose. A reader holds five to nine chunks of information at once, so one drawing with every step in it cannot be followed (Sterman, 2000): the full drawing is the overview, on one A4 page, and the loop figures and the descriptions carry the detail. What the drawing shows is set by the Purpose's question, since what makes a difference to an answer depends on the question asked (Weisberg, 2007). A step goes into words only where every loop through it keeps its polarity, reinforcing or balancing, and its mechanism, no actor's decision disappears from the drawing, each variable still measures one quantity and no delay is lost; where one of these fails, the step stays drawn, however many boxes that takes. Some simplifications leave out what is true, such as a chain of causes drawn as one link with its middle step in the description; others treat the system as simpler than it is, such as the crops of a rotation responding as one yield (Jones, 2005, pp. 174–175, 184, 187). Where an element or a link simplifies something, its panel says what is left out or distorted and why the loops through it still hold; what holds for every element is under Assumptions.

The named loops

The CLD holds 16,866 feedback loops. All of them run through one core of 53 elements and 115 links in which every element reaches every other along the links; the other 10 elements, 10 of them Constants, only drive that core. A core like this has an independent loop set of links minus elements plus one loops, here 115 − 53 + 1 = 63: a largest set of loops none of which is a combination of the others' links (Kampmann, 2012), of which Oliva (2004) takes the shortest as the simplest description of a model's feedback structure. Neither 16,866 nor 63 loops can be read in one picture. Sterman (2000) advises against putting all the loops into one large diagram: build the model up in a series of smaller diagrams, each one part of the story, and number and name the loops the story discusses so the reader can find each. The 23 named loops follow that rule: they are the loops that drive or hold back the island's farmers in adopting adaptive practices, in one or more of four ways. Some run through a practice itself, so that its own uptake speeds up or slows down. Some run through the money a farm has to adopt one: its income, its debt, the bank's terms, the capital it would lose. Some change the salt and fresh water a practice answers to. And some run through the decisions of the water board, the municipality and those objecting to their plans, which set the ditch levels and the trust behind a long investment. Each loop's description says which it does, and whether it shows an unintended consequence of a response. They are not an independent loop set, and the other loops are no less part of the system; they are the ones the story needs. The full drawing marks none of them: a loop's button in the menu highlights it and shows it on its own.

The combined loops

Some named loops belong together: they act on the same variables in a way that forms a behaviour none of them shows alone. The 10 groups below are those, not every pair of loops that shares a variable; each is named by what its loops do together and has its figure under its button in the menu, the loops joined at the variables they share. A link carries a sign and no strength, so where the loops of a group pull opposite ways the map shows the question and cannot settle it. Each link's source is on the link; what a group says beyond its links is a reading of how they work together, for the group model building session to test.

  • C1 Salt builds, and farms respond in steps (R1, B1, B2, B3). Salt in the root zone damages the soil's structure, less rain soaks in, the rainwater lens thins and more salt reaches the roots, in the low polders (R1). The responses differ in speed and cost: a tolerant variety uses the same machines and needs little capital (B1), a new crop comes after a delay (B2), and over years land leaves arable use (B3). The first two lower what salt takes from the harvest and leave the salt in the soil, so they ease the pressure that leads to the third, the one that lowers the salt, through the higher ditch levels that follow. The order is a reading of the delays and the costs, a hypothesis for the group model building session.
  • C2 Low ditch levels: workable fields now, more demand later (B4, R2). Low ditch levels dry the fields sooner, within the season, and the farmers' demand for low levels eases (B4). The same low levels raise saline seepage, the rainwater lens thins over years, salt takes more of the harvest, income and land value fall, debt payments rise and the farm depends more on its high-margin crops, whose spring work needs low levels, so the demand comes back higher (R2). The relief comes first and the extra demand years later, on the same variable. R2 comes back through debt and dependence, not through a wetter field, and the link from debt payments to dependence is a hypothesis for the group model building session.
  • C3 Debt and capital together keep a farm in its crops (R2, R3). Debt payments raise a farm's dependence on its high-margin crops (R2), and so does what it would write off on machines and stores if it switched (R3); the link from debt payments is a hypothesis for the group model building session, the one from what a switch would write off rests on sources. Each loop raises the variable that drives the other: more dependence brings more spending on machines and stores for those crops and more demand for low ditch levels, so the two add up. Dependence holds back new crops, so a farm can stay with its current crops while salt, on its own, lowers that dependence over the years.
  • C4 The quick answer to a dry summer goes first (B5, B6, B7, B8). Three loops answer one crop water shortfall at three speeds: pumping within the season (B5), storage over the years the rainwater lens takes to thicken (B6), and a supply route after the years it takes to decide, pay for and build, which has not run yet (B7). Whatever eases the shortfall first lowers the pressure for the slower answers. Pumping is tied to what it draws on: under the creek ridges and dunes, the thinner the lens, the less the water board's rules allow (B8). The quick answer and the slow one are largely on different farms: pumping where a thick lens lies under the farm, storage through the rainwater lens in the low polders, where the farmers talked to do not irrigate.
  • C5 Storing: more worthwhile, harder to pay for (R4, B6). R4 and B6 share the chain from storage measures through the rainwater lens and the shortfall to the harvest, and part ways there. A worse harvest makes storing more worthwhile to a farmer (B6), and leaves the farm less income and less to invest (R4), so the same harvest pushes storage up and down. Farms with the means store and farms without may not, though they face the same water. Which pull is stronger differs per farm: a sign cannot show it, and the group model building session can test it, both links into storage being hypotheses. It runs in the low polders.
  • C6 Salt losses raise the wish to switch and cut the means (B2, R6, B9, B10). The harvest lost to salt makes a new salt-tolerant crop worth more against the current crops, after a delay (B2). The same losses lower income and land value, so a bank sees more risk and lends less and the farm has less to invest in the switch (R6); limiting credit is how the bank keeps its own exposure down (B9). A field that moves saves harvest from salt (R6) and gives up the current crop's margin (B10), most on fresh land, so the salt on the field decides whether the switch adds income or costs it. Which of these is stronger, a sign cannot show.
  • C7 New crops stay stuck or take off (R7, B10). With few hectares under a new crop there is little harvest within reach of a plant, no local plant, and transport keeps the net price low (R7), while every field moved gives up the current crop's margin, which income and credit must carry (B10), so adoption stays low. With enough hectares a plant becomes worthwhile, the net price rises and more farmers grow the crop. A higher net price also means less margin given up, by a link outside these two loops, which eases the brake B10 puts on adoption. How many hectares that takes, a sign cannot show.
  • C8 One ditch level between four goals (B4, B11, B12, B13). Four balancing loops act on the water board's plans for the same ditch levels, each towards its own goal: fields dry enough for heavy machinery in spring (B4), the fresh water in the soil the board wants to keep (B11), the optimum the board sets for each use and soil (B12), and a plan the objecting owners can accept (B13). The goals cannot all be met at once, so the level settles where those pressures balance. How far seepage weighs against the other goals in the board's plans, no source read shows.
  • C9 Objections to one plan shape the next (B13, R8, R9, B14). Objections delay, change or stop a plan, which is what owners object for (B13). Two things follow. The levels held keep up the seepage and salt that lower the land's value, and a lower value may bring more objections (R8). And plans changed or dropped, and expropriation for a plan, lower farmers' trust in government, which may bring more objections to the next plan (R9, B14). Whether objections settle at a plan the owners accept or keep growing is a question for the group model building session, three of its steps being hypotheses: from land value to objections, from objections to trust, and from objections to the farmland planned. The trust it lowers also holds back storage, a long investment that a change of rules can strand.
  • C10 Falling land value: levels held, or land leaving arable (R2, R8, B3). The three loops share the chain from the water board's plans through the drainage depth, seepage, the rainwater lens, the salt and the harvest to income and land value, and part ways at land value. As land loses value, leverage and debt lead farmers to ask for low levels (R2) and owners may object harder to raises (R8, a hypothesis), so both keep the levels low and the salt coming. Where arable farming stops paying, land leaves arable use, the board has more reason to raise the levels, and the salt falls back (B3). The same fall sends the water board opposite signals. Which is stronger decides whether the decline runs on or stops, and a sign cannot show it.

One group has the structure of a system archetype, a pattern of loops that recurs across systems (Kim, 2000). C2 matches Fixes that fail (Kim, 2000, p. 16): a solution quickly relieves a symptom through a balancing loop (B4, the farmers' demand for low ditch levels easing), while its unintended consequences, unfolding over a long time, worsen that symptom through a reinforcing loop (R2). The name describes the structure, not anyone's decision. A group carries an archetype's name only where the loops' polarities, the variable they share and the delay all match; the others are named by what they do.

Reading the drawing

Each box is a variable, tinted by its domain in the social-ecological-technological systems (SETS) framework (McPhearson et al., 2022): Social, Ecological or Technological, so the drawing shows where loops cross from one domain to another; square corners mark a stock, a dashed border a Constant, a thick border an adaptive practice. The two faint regions are the project's two subsystems, water and soil and the agricultural economy. A blue link with + moves its effect the same way as its cause, a red link with − the opposite way; two short strokes across a link mark a delay. Click a box or a link for its description and sources. Two fingers on a trackpad or dragging moves the map, a pinch or a mouse wheel zooms, and "Fit" shows the whole drawing again.
