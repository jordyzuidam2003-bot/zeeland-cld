Crop water shortfallAuxiliaryEcologicalWater and soil

What crops need in the growing season beyond the water they get, fresh or brackish: the gap farmers' water decisions respond to. The island's farms depend wholly on rain and on the fresh groundwater under the dunes, the creek ridges and the thin rainwater lenses of the low polders; with no link to the main freshwater system, no fresh water can be brought in and no ditches flushed in dry periods, so the farms run short of water, as the dry years 2018, 2019 and 2020 showed (Kaandorp et al., 2022, p. 8). Freshwater lens rechargeAuxiliaryEcologicalWater and soil

The rain that soaks in and refills the rainwater lens: mostly the winter's rain surplus, since in summer most of the rain evaporates (van Beekom et al., 1953a, for the Netherlands). On Schouwen-Duiveland it soaks deep into the dunes and the sandy creek ridges, while in the low polders upward saline seepage keeps it within the clay top layer and tile drains carry much of it off during each rain; recharge, seepage and drainage depth together set the lens's thickness (de Louw et al., 2011, measured on the island).

de Louw et al. (2011); van Beekom et al. (1953a)
Freshwater lens thicknessStockEcologicalWater and soil

The thickness of the fresh rainwater lens on top of the saline groundwater, from the surface to the middle of the mixing zone between them. On Schouwen-Duiveland it is about 2 m on average in the low-lying polders with saline seepage, under 2 m over more than half of the 56 km² surveyed, and 5 to 15 m under the sandy creek ridges (de Louw et al., 2011, measured on the island from a helicopter and on the ground); lenses reach about 30 m under the creek ridges and the dunes, and nearly 100 m under the dunes (Kaandorp et al., 2022, p. 10). A Stock: a year's recharge barely moves it (Freshwater lens recharge → Freshwater lens thickness).

de Louw et al. (2011); Kaandorp et al. (2022)
Freshwater storage measuresStockTechnologicalWater and soil

The fresh water a year that the measures in place on the island's farms keep, store or collect for the growing season. They work three ways (Kaandorp et al., 2022, pp. 16–23, 53, for the island): in the low polders, controlled drainage, anti-salinisation drainage (Drains2Buffer) and weirs in ditches keep the thin rainwater lens against salt, the water staying in the field's own soil, where it cannot be pumped (pp. 33, 53); on the creek ridges, the dunes and where a sand layer lies under the clay, the creek-ridge infiltration system, the Freshmaker and underground storage under a clay layer infiltrate winter water to pump in summer (pp. 20–22, 38); and double drainage collects fresh drain water into a basin to irrigate from (pp. 23, 29, 32). An adaptive practice: measures to keep more fresh water in the ground as drought and salinisation increase (p. 4). The study found them not yet fitted to the island and too risky for many farms to invest in alone (pp. 4, 11). A Stock: a measure, once built, lasts 15 to 30 years (pp. 17–22, 34). The farm decides a measure; infiltrating water needs the water board's permit or a notification, a weir the neighbours' consent, and raising a ditch's level the water board's cooperation (pp. 26, 44–45). Whether a farm wants to store weighs storing against its other ways through a dry summer (pumping, brackish water, other crops), where capacity to invest is whether it can. Stored water pays back over years, which suits a farm that owns its land more than one on a short lease; and where stored water spreads under the neighbours, one farm pays and others gain. Whether either holds a farm back is a question for the group model building session.

Simplification: Three ways in one element: keeping the low polders' rainwater lens, infiltrating winter water to pump on the creek ridges and the dunes, and collecting drain water in a basin, counted together as the fresh water they keep, store or collect. Each way adds fresh water and none takes it away, so the sum keeps the sign of every link into and out of it, and where a link works through one way only, its description says which. What one element leaves out: the ways work in different places, through different routes and under different permits and consent, which the description names. Arable land farmed by its owner, a Constant that made storing more worthwhile, is in the description instead: no loop runs through a Constant, so none changes, and the share cannot be set as an input of its own.

Groundwater extraction for irrigationAuxiliaryTechnologicalWater and soil

Fresh water farmers pump from the fresh groundwater through their own wells, to irrigate or to flush their fields in summer; the water board's pumping stations belong to drainage. The water board sets the rules: a horizontal well at most 6 m below the surface and 80 to 200 m long, at least 200 m from the next well and 50 m from a neighbour's field, pumping at most 800 m³ per hectare and 8,000 m³ per well a year (Waterschap Scheldestromen, 2026b); in Zeeland only from fresh groundwater more than 15 m thick, outside vulnerable areas (Kaandorp et al., 2022, p. 26). Whose lens a well draws on, the farm's own or one it shares with its neighbours, so that a farm pumping less leaves water to them, is a hypothesis for the group model building session; the rules are written so that a well's effects stay within the farm's own field (Gedeputeerde Staten van Zeeland, 2021), an aim of the rules rather than a measurement. Pumping follows the season's shortfall within the year: an Auxiliary.

Harvest lost to saltAuxiliaryEcologicalWater and soil

The conventional crops' harvest that salt in the root zone takes, over the island's fields still under them, in a season. On the island, farms where seepage pressure is high and the surface water is salty all year have stopped growing sensitive crops such as sown onions (Kaandorp et al., 2022, p. 31). Zeeland is among the coastal provinces where potatoes and barley lose considerable yield to salt (Deolu-Ajayi et al., 2024, p. 12). Salt's effect on yield builds up over weeks to months and is read over the season (Allen et al., 1998, ch. 8): within the year, so an Auxiliary.

Polder drainage depthStockTechnologicalWater and soil

How far below the surface the water board holds the ditch levels in the island's polders (the drooglegging), as its water level plans set them: Duiveland's plan sets a target level for each of its level areas, from 0.8 to 2.7 m below NAP, in most the summer level higher than the winter one, against nature's order, to prevent water damage in winter and drying in summer (Waterschap Scheldestromen, 2021a, pp. 37, 63–64). It moves from the winter level to the summer one in steps in March and April, as the weather and the farmers' soil work allow (p. 66). On a creek ridge studied for water storage the depth is over 1.5 m in winter in large parts (Kaandorp et al., 2022, p. 42). The depth holds until the board carries out a new plan: Duiveland's plan of 2021 replaced one from 1982 (Waterschap Scheldestromen, 2021a, p. 24), and the board checks each plan every three years and renews it at 17 years or once it no longer fits (Waterschap Scheldestromen, 2025, pp. 16–17), so a Stock. Where the land subsides under a level held, the depth shrinks until a new plan lowers the level, as the board has done where there is peat (2025, p. 13).

Kaandorp et al. (2022); Waterschap Scheldestromen (2021a, 2025)
Polder surface elevation (NAP)StockEcologicalWater and soil

Physical land surface elevation of the polder (-1.5m to -3.5m NAP), lowered by subsidence. Tidal silt, let in through a wisselpolder, would barely raise it on this island: the Grevelingen has no tide, and the Oosterschelde has too little silt to raise polders on a large scale (De Mesel et al., 2013). Since the storm surge barrier its channels carry 52–70% less suspended sediment (Ma et al., 2014), under 10 mg/l near Zierikzee, and its salt marshes rise 3–10 mm a year (van Maldegem & de Jong, 2004), against 16–32 mm on young marshes along the Westerschelde (De Mesel et al., 2013).

De Mesel et al. (2013); Ma et al. (2014); van Maldegem & de Jong (2004)
Root zone salinityAuxiliaryEcologicalWater and soil

The salt concentration of the soil water in the crop root zone. Salt harms a crop in two phases: at once osmotically, slowing the growth of young leaves, and more slowly as sodium (Na+) or chloride (Cl-) builds up in the leaves, ageing the older ones early (Munns & Tester, 2008, a review of plant physiology, which holds wherever the crop grows). It rises in a dry summer as the soil dries out and salt rises by capillary rise, and falls with the winter's rain surplus, which washes the topsoil of well-drained soil free of salt in one winter and that of badly drained soil in two or three (van Beekom et al., 1953a, measured after the floods in Zeeland): within the year where the soil drains well, so an Auxiliary.

Munns & Tester (2008); van Beekom et al. (1953a)
Saline seepage (kwel)AuxiliaryEcologicalWater and soil

The upward flow of salt groundwater from the sand below into the clay and peat top layer of the island's polders, in mm a day, which leaves through drains, ditches and evaporation (de Louw et al., 2011, p. 3662). The higher water pressure at depth drives it in the low polders, where it keeps the rainwater lenses thin, mostly under 2 m, and the shallow groundwater brackish to salt (Kaandorp et al., 2022, p. 10); below the lens its water holds 10 to 16 g of chloride a litre (de Louw et al., 2011, p. 3675), against about 16 to 17 g in North Sea water (Kaandorp et al., 2022, p. 10). Most of Duiveland gets salt seepage, most in its low parts near the Oosterschelde (Waterschap Scheldestromen, 2021a, p. 27); in the western low polders it is strongest near the Grevelingen and the Oosterschelde (Kaandorp et al., 2022, p. 27). The flow follows the water pressures, which change constantly with recharge and drainage (de Louw et al., 2011, p. 3675): within the year, so an Auxiliary.

Soil structureStockEcologicalWater and soil

The soil's aggregates and pores, which set how fast water soaks in: well-drained clay with its structure intact let the winter's rain surplus soak through almost completely, while clay whose structure sodium had broken down became impervious and sticky when wet (van Beekom et al., 1953a, 1953b, measured after the floods in Zeeland). A Stock: once broken down, it recovers over years (Root zone salinity → Soil structure). Workability, which structure and wetness both shape, is Spring workability of the fields.

Whether heavy machinery can work the fields early in spring without damaging the soil, set by soil structure and by how wet the soil is: soil work done when the soil is too wet smears and compacts it and forms clods, and more rain in spring leaves fewer moments to do it (Verstand et al., 2021, p. 25, from the Dutch cultivation guides, on potatoes). It follows the soil's wetness within the season, so an Auxiliary.

Water board's plans to raise ditch levelsStockSocialWater and soil

The water level plans in which Waterschap Scheldestromen raises a polder's ditch levels, from the draft until their levels take effect. The board matches its levels to the land's use and soil, its principle that the level follows the land use: arable land 80 to 120 cm below the surface by soil, permanent grassland 20 cm less (Waterschap Scheldestromen, 2021a, p. 29; 2025, pp. 5, 7), weighing flooding, drought, fresh water and groundwater (2021c, p. 19), subsidence (2025, pp. 13–14) and a change's costs against its benefits (2025, p. 12). On Duiveland the plan of 2021 proposed studying a raise in 14 level areas, where levels had been kept low to store extreme rain, to enlarge the fresh water in the soil and give crops more moisture (2021a, p. 69). The general board adopts each plan (2025, p. 18); of its 30 members, 26 represent the residents and two the owners of unbuilt land, farmland included, appointed by ZLTO's board (Reglement voor het Waterschap Scheldestromen, 2025, arts. 6–7). A plan waits years for the works its levels need (Water board's plans to raise ditch levels → Polder drainage depth), so a Stock.

Simplification: The two seats on the general board for the owners of unbuilt land, farmland included, were a Constant driving these plans, and are in the description instead: no loop runs through a Constant, so no loop changes. What it leaves out: the seats cannot be set as an input of their own.

Root zone salinityHarvest lost to salt+no

Once a crop's root zone salinity, as the conductivity of the saturated soil extract (ECe), passes its threshold, its yield falls in proportion to the excess (Allen et al., 1998, ch. 8, eq. 89): onions lose 16% per dS/m above 1.2 dS/m and potatoes 12% above 1.7, rated sensitive and moderately sensitive, against 5.9% above 7.0 for sugar beet and 7.1% above 6.0 for wheat (Table 23), so on the island's rotations salt takes most from potatoes and onions. The harm is osmotic at once and comes from sodium or chloride building up in the leaves more slowly (Munns & Tester, 2008). Table 23 is a guideline from experiments mostly under irrigation, tolerance varying with climate, soil and practice, and crops are often less tolerant as they germinate (Table 23, note 1): crop physiology that holds on the island's fields, its thresholds not measured there. Within the season.

Allen et al. (1998); Munns & Tester (2008)
Root zone salinitySoil structure−yes

Salt in the root zone puts sodium (Na+) on the clay in place of calcium (Ca2+); once fresh water washes the salt out, clay that holds much sodium disperses: its aggregates and pores collapse, with troubles to be expected above 1 m.e. of exchangeable sodium per 100 g of dry soil. The delay is on the way back: structure collapses within the first winter after the salt leaches out, but the clay only recovers as calcium replaces the sodium again, about 0.7 m.e. Na per 100 g a year, which takes 5 to 10 years on heavy clay and about a year on light soil (van Beekom et al., 1953b, after the 1945 and 1953 floods in Zeeland). Seawater flooding is a much larger salt dose than seepage; soils that took up less sodium recover faster, and no source read shows how much sodium the island's seepage puts on its clay.

Simplification: Folds Sodium on the clay, a stock between the two: the salt puts sodium on the clay, and the sodium breaks the clay's structure once the salt washes out. The sign is the product of the two steps (+, then −), so no loop through the link changes, and the stock's slowness is the link's delay. What one link distorts: the delay lies on the way back only, the structure collapsing within the first winter after the salt leaches out and recovering over years, while the link draws one delay for both ways.

The water board names the gradually increasing salinisation a concern for the fresh water farming needs, the thin fresh groundwater lenses most threatened, and aims to keep or enlarge the lenses: by not lowering target levels much, since a lower level lowers the counter-pressure on the salt groundwater and raises the seepage pressure, while a raise lowers it (Waterschap Scheldestromen, 2021a, p. 37, a criterion of its analysis for Duiveland's water level plan).
