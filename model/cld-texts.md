## Purpose

The group's research question: "Which systemic barriers hold back the adaptation to increasing salinisation and freshwater scarcity of arable farming in Schouwen-Duiveland towards 2050, and which levers could the actors in this system use to overcome these barriers?" The CLD is the system-wide map of its first part, and with it of the second sub-question: which feedback loops, trade-offs and socio-economic conditions drive or hold back the island's farmers in adopting adaptive practices as soil and water get saltier and fresh water scarcer, and why farms facing similar salt and water conditions respond differently from one another. The responses in the model are: carrying on with the current crops and drainage, storing fresh water on the farm, caring for the soil, growing crops that cope with salt or with little fresh water, bringing in fresh water from outside, and land leaving arable farming. Which feedback loops make one response more likely than another, and for whom? The adaptive practices in the model come in three families, each working on a different part of the chain and each held back by something in the system: water (storing fresh water and pumping it), soil (caring for its structure) and crops (new crops and tolerant varieties that cope with salt or with little fresh water). A farm practice beyond these enters where a source shows its effect on the island's fields and a partner names it as a choice the island's farmers make; what makes a farmer choose it is then a hypothesis for the group model building session to test.

The CLD is the first step of the study. In the group model building session on the island, farmers and regional stakeholders build their own map of the subsystems from scratch; comparing it with this one shows what we missed or misjudged, and the two are combined. The combined CLD then goes into the semi-quantitative analysis (D2D), which compares single policy levers and combinations of them by their effect on an outcome and traces their unintended effects, and into the multi-criteria analysis of the policy options. It is not built around one outcome or one set of levers: the group picks those when that step starts.

## The vision

Schouwen-Duiveland farms on a thin layer of fresh water over salt, with no fresh water coming in from outside. Climate change squeezes that layer from both sides: more salt from below, less water from above. Salt also spoils water that's there, in the ditches and at the wells. The crops that earn the most need the most water and stand salt the least. Farmers respond, but each response hits a limit. The quick answers, deep drainage and pumping, make the water problem worse later. Storage is small against the gap. Switching crops needs a market, and the farm's debts and machines hold it to its current crops. What would close the gap is larger than one farm: a supply route, storage at scale, a chain for other crops. Nor is it the farmers' alone to decide. The water board sets the ditch levels and routes the water, the banks decide on credit, the buyers on contracts and plants, the province on a supply route, the municipality on where a basin may be built. Each decides from its own interest, and none is responsible for the whole. So the barriers are mostly collective, while the risk of moving lands on the single farmer.

## Spatial scale

The island of Schouwen-Duiveland, the island of Zeeland the research zooms in on to keep it feasible: its farmland polders, the rainwater lenses under them, the fresh groundwater under its creek ridges and dunes, the soils, the farms and the land market, and the decisions of the bodies that act there (the water board's water levels, levy and rules on pumping, the province's supply route, the municipality's rules for water basins, the banks' lending, the buyers and processors of its crops). The island gets no fresh water from elsewhere, so all its fresh water is winter rain stored in the lenses. What is decided at national and EU level (LVVN, IenW and Rijkswaterstaat, the Deltaprogramma Zoetwater, EU banking rules) comes from outside the boundary: it enters as a Constant or a policy input, and the CLD shows how it reaches the island.

## Time horizon

2050, the year the KNMI'23 climate scenarios and the project look ahead to. The slow parts of the system are judged against that date: the freshwater lenses, which a year's recharge barely moves, and land subsidence, which lowers the polders by millimetres a year. The time unit is a year, for every part of the CLD. Each link's description says how long its effect takes to show, from a source, in bands: within the year, years, a decade or more, or beyond 2050; a link slower than a year carries a delay. The seasons sit inside the year, named in the variables they shape (hotter, drier summers; wetter winters; winter rain drained away). Heavier summer downpours have no variable of their own: they enter through Farmers' demand for deep polder drainage, whose description carries them, since a variable of their own would add no feedback loop, no actor's decision and no delay. A variable that something outside the island sets, or that moves too little by 2050 to matter, is a Constant.

## System boundary

The test, from the Purpose (the group's): inside if the farms' responses move it and it moves them back before 2050; outside if it moves the responses and the island cannot move it; left out if it does not move the responses by 2050. Inside the model (endogenous, explained by its loops): the water and salt system (the rainwater lenses of the low polders and the lens under the creek ridges and dunes, seepage, upconing, salt in the root zone and the ditch water, soil structure); crop yield and farm finances (revenue, land value, debt, credit, room to invest); the farmers' decisions (the drainage they ask for, pumping, storing water, new crops and tolerant varieties, spending on keeping the current crops); and the decisions of the other actors inside the scale: the water board's ditch levels, levy and rules on pumping, the banks' lending, the buyers' contracts and processing capacity for new crops, Agrarisch Schouwen-Duiveland's call for a supply route and the province's supply route, with the objections and the trust around the water board's plans. Outside the model (exogenous: they drive it, the model does not explain them): the climate (hotter, drier summers, wetter winters, sea-level rise), EU banking rules (Basel IV, in the description of the risk premium on farm loans), market demand for alternative crops, world prices of conventional crops (what carrying on is weighed against), the growing and processing of new crops elsewhere, the salt-tolerant varieties on offer, the trials and pilots on the island, the municipality's rules for water basins outside a building plot (whether they hold storage back is a question for the group model building session), national water policy and LVVN's levers. The arable land in the polders is in the model as a stock, which falls when farms stop and their land leaves arable farming, because land value and succession drive it and water management follows it; what the land becomes afterwards is not. Left out: nature and energy, and other land uses (livestock, vertical farming), which replace arable farming rather than adapt it; wisselpolders, because the island's waters carry too little silt to raise its polders (see Polder surface elevation); and salt intrusion into estuaries and irrigation with brackish water, because no water reaches the island's ditches from outside and no source read shows its farmers irrigating with brackish water. Left out as well are the adaptation options that a study of the island's freshwater need finds far from practice there or of little help against a lack of water: agroforestry and strip cropping, still experimental and far from practice, switching to them very intensive, and the wide strips already on the island needing no less water, as a strip-cropping trial at Rusthoeve was no less sensitive to drought; organic farming, which needs no less fresh water (Delphy, 2023, p. 27); and good soil care, which helps fields withstand heavy showers as well as drought but can never replace water: by a meta-analysis it cites, 1% more organic matter holds at most 4 mm more water, and raising it by 1% takes five to ten years or longer for most crop rotations (Delphy, 2023, p. 28). Crops that need less water are in the model as far as farms keep fewer of the crops that need it when fresh water runs short (B15, The water sets the crops). We assume the adaptive practices work rather than model whether they do: the CLD shows what stops farmers adopting them, not whether each one works on the island.

## Assumptions

- Each variable is a total for the whole island, and the island's arable farmers are one actor; a group of farms gets its own variables only where a link works differently for it and a source shows that on the island.
- Each variable measures one quantity, named in its description, so the sign of every link into it can be read.
- Each actor inside the spatial scale enters the CLD through the variables it decides, with the drivers it responds to; the actors outside it act on the island from outside.
- A link that claims why an actor decides as it does, and has no source yet, is a hypothesis for the group model building session on the island: its panel says so, and the drawing does not mark it. Every other link is to rest on a source.
- A rate measured elsewhere in the delta holds for the island only where its place shares the mechanism's driver: the island borders the Oosterschelde and the Grevelingen, whose tide and silt differ from the Westerschelde's.
- The adaptive practices are assumed to work: the CLD shows what stops farmers adopting them, not whether each one works on the island.
- The labels and descriptions describe what happens in words each actor in it would accept, and no variable is marked as anyone's lever: which outcome and which levers the analysis looks at is the group's choice for that step.

## Simplifications

The CLD leaves detail out on purpose. A reader holds five to nine chunks of information at once, so one drawing with every loop in it is hard to follow (Sterman, 2000, p. 154): the full drawing is the overview, on one A4 page, and the loop figures and the descriptions carry the detail. What the drawing shows is set by the Purpose's question, since what makes a difference to an answer depends on the question asked (Weisberg, 2007, sec. II). A step goes into words only where every loop through it keeps its polarity, reinforcing or balancing, and its mechanism, no actor's decision disappears from the drawing, each variable still measures one quantity and no delay is lost; where one of these fails, the step stays drawn, however many boxes that takes. Some simplifications leave out what is true, such as a chain of causes drawn as one link with its middle step in the description; others treat the system as simpler than it is, such as the crops of a rotation responding as one yield (Frigg & Hartmann, 2026, sec. 1). Where an element or a link simplifies something, its panel says what is left out or distorted and why the loops through it still hold; what holds for every element is under Assumptions.

## The named loops

The CLD holds {feedback loops} feedback loops. All of them run through one core of {core elements} elements and {core links} links in which every element reaches every other along the links; the other {other elements} elements, {other constants} of them Constants, only drive that core. A core like this has an independent loop set of links minus elements plus one loops, here {core links} − {core elements} + 1 = {independent loops}: a largest set of loops none of which is a combination of the others' links (Kampmann, 2012), of which Oliva (2004) takes the shortest as the simplest description of a model's feedback structure. Neither {feedback loops} nor {independent loops} loops can be read in one picture. Sterman (2000) advises against putting all the loops into one large diagram: build the model up in a series of smaller diagrams, each one part of the story, and number and name the loops the story discusses so the reader can find each. The {named loops} named loops follow that rule: they are the loops that drive or hold back the island's farmers in adopting adaptive practices, in one or more of four ways. Some run through a practice itself, so that its own uptake speeds up or slows down. Some run through the money a farm has to adopt one: its income, its debt, the bank's terms, the capital it would lose. Some change the salt and fresh water a practice answers to. And some run through the decisions of the water board and those objecting to its plans, which set the ditch levels and the trust behind a long investment. Each loop's description says which it does, and whether it shows an unintended consequence of a response. They are not an independent loop set, and the other loops are no less part of the system; they are the ones the story needs. The full drawing marks none of them: a loop's button in the menu highlights it and shows it on its own.

## The combined loops

Some named loops belong together: they act on the same variables in a way that forms a behaviour none of them shows alone. The {combined loops} groups below are those, not every pair of loops that shares a variable; each is named by what its loops do together and has its figure under its button in the menu, the loops joined at the variables they share. A link carries a sign and no strength, so where the loops of a group pull opposite ways the map shows the question and cannot settle it. Each link's source is on the link; what a group says beyond its links is a reading of how they work together, for the group model building session to test.

One group has the structure of a system archetype, a pattern of loops that recurs across systems (Kim, 2000). C2 matches Fixes that fail (Kim, 2000, p. 16): a solution quickly relieves a symptom through a balancing loop (B4, the farmers' demand for low ditch levels easing), while its unintended consequences, unfolding over a long time, worsen that symptom through a reinforcing loop (R2). The name describes the structure, not anyone's decision. A group carries an archetype's name only where the loops' polarities, the variable they share and the delay all match; the others are named by what they do.

## Reading the drawing

Each box is a variable, tinted by its domain in the social-ecological-technological systems (SETS) framework (McPhearson et al., 2022): Social, Ecological or Technological, so the drawing shows where loops cross from one domain to another; square corners mark a stock, a dashed border a Constant, a thick border an adaptive practice. The two faint regions are the project's two subsystems, water and soil and the agricultural economy. A blue link with + moves its effect the same way as its cause, a red link with − the opposite way; two short strokes across a link mark a delay. Click a box or a link for its description and sources.
